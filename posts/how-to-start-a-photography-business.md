Starting a photography business takes less than most people expect, but the order you do things in matters. The short version: decide what you shoot, build a small portfolio, handle the business basics, price your work properly, use a contract, find your first clients, and set up simple systems before you get busy.

## The short answer

1. Pick one type of photography to lead with.
2. Build a small portfolio of your best work in that area.
3. Get the gear you need for the work you are actually booking.
4. Handle the legal, insurance, and tax basics.
5. Price for the business, not just the shoot.
6. Use a contract for every job.
7. Get your first clients from the people around you.
8. Set up simple systems for inquiries, payments, and delivery.

## 1. Choose what you will shoot first

You can shoot more than one thing eventually. At the start, lead with one: weddings, portraits, families, newborns, events, or products. Clients hire the photographer who looks like the obvious choice for their situation, and a focused portfolio makes that easy.

## 2. Build a portfolio you can actually show

You do not need a hundred images. A small, consistent set of your strongest work in your chosen area is better than a large mixed collection. If you do not have client work yet, shoot friends, family, or models for free in exchange for permission to use the photos. Treat these as portfolio sessions, not favors.

## 3. Get the gear you need, not the gear you want

Many photographers start with the camera they already own. Add equipment when paying work shows you what is limiting you. A reliable camera, a lens or two that fit your style, spare batteries and memory cards, and a backup plan for your files matter more than the newest body.

## 4. Handle the business basics

This is the part people skip, and it is the part that protects you.

- **Legal setup:** many photographers operate as a sole proprietor or form an LLC. The right choice depends on where you live.
- **Licenses and registration:** some places require a business registration or license.
- **Insurance:** liability and equipment insurance are common and sometimes required by venues.
- **Taxes:** set aside part of every payment and keep your records clean from day one.

Rules vary by location, and this is general information, not legal or tax advice. A local accountant is worth an hour of your time here.

## 5. Price for the business, not just the shoot

A common beginner mistake is pricing only for the hours behind the camera. Your price has to cover the time before and after the shoot (communication, planning, editing, delivery), your gear, your software, your insurance, your taxes, and the unpaid time you spend running the business. Pricing too low at the start is hard to undo later.

## 6. Use a contract for every job

A contract protects you and the client. It should cover what you will deliver, the price, the payment schedule, a deposit or retainer, what happens if the date changes or is canceled, and how the photos can be used. Our guide on [how to write a wedding photography contract](/blog/how-to-write-a-wedding-photography-contract) covers the main sections, and most of it applies to any type of shoot.

## 7. Get your first clients

Your first clients usually come from people close to you, not from ads.

- Tell friends, family, and coworkers what you shoot, and ask them to pass it on.
- Introduce yourself to local vendors who work near your clients, such as venues, planners, and florists.
- Ask every happy client for a referral and a review.
- Make sure there is a place online where someone can see your work and contact you.

## 8. Set up simple systems early

Every client goes through the same path: an inquiry, a booking, a contract, payments, the shoot, and delivery. The more of that path you handle by hand, the more time it takes as you get busier. Many photographers use a client-management tool, a portfolio website, and a gallery service. We cover the main options in [what tools photographers need to run their business](/blog/tools-every-photographer-needs-to-run-their-business).

## Where to start this week

Pick your one focus, gather your best 10 to 20 images in it, set up one place online to show them, and tell five people what you are doing. That is enough to get your first inquiry.
